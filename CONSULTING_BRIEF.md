# Consulting Brief: EBMUD Contract Equity Program Benchmarking Engagement

**Client:** East Bay Municipal Utility District (EBMUD)  
**Program:** Contract Equity Program (CEP)  
**Engagement Type:** Comparative Benchmarking & Gap Analysis  
**Deliverables:** Executive assessment, peer-sourced recommendations, implementation roadmap

---

## Executive Summary

EBMUD's Contract Equity Program has sound foundational design (4.38/5.0) but faces critical execution gaps in three areas: implementation capacity (3.57/5.0, -0.74 vs. peer average), supplier engagement (3.70/5.0, -0.57 vs. peer average), and economic impact measurement (1.25/5.0, -2.45 vs. peer average). A comparative benchmarking analysis against 8 peer utilities identified specific, peer-sourced recommendations to close these gaps, organized by priority and implementation complexity.

**Recommendation:** Pursue a phased 18–24 month transformation addressing the three gap areas in sequence, with quarterly Board updates and an external advisory council to guide implementation.

### Critical Gaps: Root Cause Analysis

**Gap 1: Economic & Community Impact (-2.45 points) — CRITICAL**

*The Symptom:*
- EBMUD: 1.25/5.0 (no economic impact quantification)
- Peer average: 3.70/5.0
- Peer leaders (PG&E, SCE, SDG&E): 4.15–5.00/5.0

*The Cause:*
- No commissioned economic impact study (PG&E, SCE, SDG&E all have annual studies)
- No supplier success stories or case studies published
- No tracking of supplier business outcomes (revenue growth, employment, capacity building)
- Internal data systems lack the detail needed for impact analysis

*The Implication:*
- Leadership cannot demonstrate program ROI to ratepayers or Board
- EBMUD appears to lack economic justification for program investments
- Missed opportunity for stakeholder engagement and political support
- Program framed as compliance exercise, not business development tool

*The Fix (Peer-Sourced):*
- PG&E approach: Partner with UCLA for annual third-party study (~$50–75K/year); quantifies jobs, regional output, tax revenue
- SCE approach: Independent economist partnership; tracks supplier progression and capacity growth
- WSSC Water approach: Annual impact narrative with supplier retention metrics (lower-cost alternative)

---

**Gap 2: Implementation & Operations (-0.74 points) — HIGH**

*The Symptom:*
- EBMUD: 3.57/5.0 (2–3 dedicated FTE, limited supplier development programming, manual reporting)
- Peer average: 4.31/5.0
- Peer leaders (Southwest Gas, PG&E, SCE): 4.62–5.00/5.0

*The Cause:*
- Understaffing (2–3 FTE vs. 6–10 at top peers)
- No dedicated data analyst or reporting infrastructure
- Compliance-driven rather than strategy-driven (reactively respond to issues vs. proactively manage)
- Limited internal training on program importance

*The Implication:*
- Supplier development programming inconsistent (1–2 events/year vs. 18–22 at top peers)
- Real-time data unavailable (can't quickly answer "what % of suppliers advanced to prime contractor status?")
- Cross-departmental procurement units lack accountability for diversity goals
- Program operates below senior leadership visibility

*The Fix (Peer-Sourced):*
- WSSC Water approach: Dedicated Office of Supplier Diversity & Inclusion with 6 FTE; clear role specialization (MBE manager, outreach coordinator, compliance analyst)
- PG&E approach: 8+ team with specialized tracks; "SupplierOne" enterprise platform for real-time dashboards to Board
- Southwest Gas approach: Real-time dashboard integration; quarterly compliance reviews with all procurement units

---

**Gap 3: Outreach & Accessibility (-0.57 points) — MEDIUM**

*The Symptom:*
- EBMUD: 3.70/5.0 (1–2 annual events, basic online resources, no supplier council)
- Peer average: 4.27/5.0
- Peer leaders (SCE, SDG&E, PG&E): 4.70–5.00/5.0

*The Cause:*
- Limited staff capacity (understaffing problem, above)
- No supplier advisory council (creates information asymmetry; EBMUD doesn't hear supplier needs)
- Professional development inconsistent (no structured "bootcamp" for emerging firms)
- Minimal business development resources online (no certification guide, no bid templates, no "how to do business with EBMUD" toolkit)

*The Implication:*
- Suppliers don't know about opportunities or how to bid
- Underrepresented firms face higher barriers to entry
- EBMUD doesn't receive feedback on program effectiveness or pain points
- Program lacks trust/credibility in supplier community

*The Fix (Peer-Sourced):*
- SCE approach: 98-member Supplier Diversity Advisory Council (quarterly meetings, published recommendations); 22 workshops/year across tiered levels (Basics → Bootcamp → Prime Readiness Academy)
- SDG&E approach: Supplier retention focus; quarterly business reviews with top 50 diverse suppliers
- PG&E approach: Comprehensive online resource center (downloadable toolkits, video tutorials, searchable event calendar)

## Prioritized Recommendations

### Priority 1: Commission Independent Economic Impact Study
**Dimension:** Economic & Community Impact  
**Gap:** -2.45 points (largest)  
**Complexity:** High  
**Timeline:** 6–12 months  

**What It Is:**
A third-party study quantifying jobs, wages, economic output, and community impact resulting from EBMUD's diverse supplier spending. Published annually.

**Why Now:**
- Largest single gap; demonstrates least maturity
- Easiest to show leadership value (concrete numbers)
- Foundation for Phase 2 roadmap (if economic case is strong, justifies investment)

**Peer Examples:**
- **PG&E:** UCLA Anderson partnership; $1.1B annual economic output; 4,600 jobs supported; multi-year trend analysis
- **SCE:** Independent economist; 6,500 jobs; $1.2B output; supplier progression tracking
- **SDG&E:** Beacon Economics; $740M regional economic activity; 3,400 jobs

**Implementation Path:**
1. **Months 1–2:** Issue RFP to 3–5 economic consulting firms (Beacon, BBC Research, UCLA, etc.)
2. **Months 3–4:** Establish partnership; firm requests data from EBMUD (budget, supplier spend by category, headcount data)
3. **Months 5–10:** Firm conducts analysis using input-output modeling (IMPLAN or REMI multipliers)
4. **Months 11–12:** Final report + Board presentation + press release

**Investment:** $30–50K per year (contracted); cost recoverable via stakeholder confidence and political support

**Expected Outcome:** "EBMUD's diverse supplier program supported X jobs and generated $Y in regional economic activity in FY24"

---

### Priority 2: Establish Supplier Advisory Council + Expand Professional Development
**Dimension:** Outreach & Accessibility  
**Gap:** -0.57 points  
**Complexity:** Medium  
**Timeline:** 3–6 months  

**What It Is:**
- **Supplier Advisory Council:** Standing body of 15–25 diverse supplier representatives; quarterly meetings; structured feedback on program barriers, recommendations for improvements
- **Professional Development Series:** Quarterly workshops progressing from basics to advanced (Doing Business 101 → Bid Readiness Bootcamp → Prime Contractor Academy)

**Why Now:**
- Medium-sized gap; improves supplier pipeline and EBMUD's understanding of what's working/not
- Complements Priority 1 (advisors provide input to economic study; become program advocates)
- Improves supplier retention and satisfaction

**Peer Examples:**
- **SCE:** 98-member Supplier Diversity Advisory Council; quarterly meetings since 2012; published recommendations annually
- **SDG&E:** Quarterly business reviews with top 50 suppliers; "Early Payment" pilot (15-day payment vs. standard 30–45 days)
- **PG&E:** 22 annual workshops; tiered curricula (Basics → Bootcamp → Prime Academy); 340+ annual participants

**Implementation Path:**
1. **Month 1:** Identify and recruit 15–25 diverse supplier representatives (balance of prime and sub, multiple industries, geographic spread)
2. **Month 2:** Hold inaugural council meeting; establish charter (goals, meeting cadence, decision authority)
3. **Months 3–6:** Host first round of professional development workshops (4 sessions across Year 1)
4. **Ongoing:** Quarterly council meetings; annual program review

**Investment:** $20–30K annually (staff time, workshop venues, light catering); low incremental cost if paired with existing events

**Expected Outcome:** "EBMUD hosted 4 professional development workshops (150+ attendees) and established a 20-member Supplier Advisory Council that meets quarterly to inform program improvements"

**Why Now:**
- Enables Priorities 1 & 2 (data analyst supports economic study; outreach specialist manages advisory council and workshops)
- Closes visibility gap (leadership can't track progress without real-time data)
- Demonstrates EBMUD's commitment to program maturity

**Peer Examples:**

- **SCE:** 10-person Supplier Diversity Team; enterprise platform; annual performance incentives for procurement staff tied to diversity goals

**Implementation Path:**
1. **Month 1:** Develop job descriptions; identify hiring budget
2. **Months 2–3:** Recruit and hire Data Analyst and Outreach Specialist
3. **Months 2–6:** Dashboard vendor evaluation; SELECT: Salesforce, Workday, or homegrown (Python/Tableau) solution

## Phase 2 Blueprint: AI-Enabled Transformation Roadmap

### Overview

If EBMUD approves Phase 1 recommendations, Phase 2 could build on the benchmarking foundation to design a multi-year AI-transformation roadmap addressing three strategic objectives:

1. **Automate Evidence Collection** (currently manual)
2. **Predict Supplier Success** (identify high-potential diverse firms early)
3. **Optimize Supplier Matching** (match prime contractors with qualified subcontractors)

### Phase 2 Scope (18–24 months)

| Initiative | Problem Solved | Opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| **AI-Powered Evidence Extraction** | Manual scoring takes 100+ hours; uses LLM to auto-extract and classify supplier info from public documents | Reduce scoring cycle from weeks to days; scale to quarterly updates |
| **Supplier Success Prediction** | Identified high-potential suppliers opportunistically; no systematic early identification | ML model predicts which new suppliers will advance to prime contractor status; proactive mentorship |
| **Supplier Matching Engine** | Prime contractors manually search for qualified subcontractors; mismatches common | Match algorithm surfaces ideal subcontractor candidates (capability, capacity, location); increase utilization |
| **Governance Framework** | No formal AI governance; risk of bias in model outputs | Build responsible AI review checklist; audit model predictions quarterly for fairness |

### Why This Matters

The benchmarking analysis (Phase 1) identifies *what* EBMUD needs to improve (economic impact, outreach, staffing). Phase 2 addresses *how* to scale these improvements efficiently using AI, turning insights into business outcomes.

**Example:** Economic Impact Study (Priority 1) requires quarterly supplier data updates. Phase 2 could automate data collection via LLM, reducing manual effort 80% and enabling real-time impact tracking.

## Conclusion

EBMUD's Contract Equity Program is well-designed but under-executed. The gap between program design (4.38) and execution (3.57 avg) reflects understaffing and limited supplier engagement, not flawed strategy.
